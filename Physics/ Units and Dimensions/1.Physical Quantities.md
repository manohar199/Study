### PART 2: SUBTOPIC LESSON: UNITS AND DIMENSIONS
#### Subtopic 1.1: Introduction & Physical Quantity
---

#### 1. Concept

Physics is the study of the fundamental laws of nature and how they govern the universe. To understand natural phenomena, describe physical events, and formulate physical laws, physicists rely on quantitative measurements.

A physical quantity is any property of a material, object, or phenomenon that can be measured directly or indirectly using a scientific instrument and expressed quantitatively.

---

#### 2. Definition

- **Physical Quantity:** Any property of a system or object that can be measured and expressed in terms of a numerical value and a unit. Examples include mass, length, time, velocity, force, electric charge, and temperature.
- **Unit:** A chosen standard of reference of the same physical nature, used to compare and measure a given physical quantity.
- **Numerical Value \(n\):** The magnitude representing how many times the standard unit is contained in the measured physical quantity.

---

#### 3. Physical Meaning

When we state that the distance between two points is \(5\text{ m}\), the number 5 indicates that the length is 5 times the chosen standard unit of length (the metre).

The physical magnitude of a quantity \(Q\) remains invariant regardless of the system of units used for its measurement:

\[
\text{Magnitude of Quantity }(Q) = \text{Numerical Value }(n) \times \text{Unit }(u)
\]

If a physical quantity is measured in two different systems of units with units \(u_1\) and \(u_2\), having corresponding numerical values \(n_1\) and \(n_2\):

\[
Q = n_1u_1 = n_2u_2
\]

This demonstrates an inverse relationship:

\[
n \propto \frac{1}{u} \quad \Rightarrow \quad n\cdot u = \text{constant}
\]

**Physical significance:** The larger the size of the chosen unit \(u\), the smaller the numerical value \(n\) required to express the magnitude of the quantity.

---

#### 4. Law / Principle

- **Principle of Measurement Invariance:** The actual physical magnitude of a physical quantity \(Q\) is independent of the choice of unit system used to measure it.
- **Rule of Quantifiability:** A concept becomes a physical quantity if and only if it can be assigned an operational procedure for measurement and an unambiguous definition.

---

#### 5. Equation

\[
Q = n\cdot u
\]

For interconversion between two systems:

\[
n_1u_1 = n_2u_2
\]

\[
n_2 = n_1\left(\frac{u_1}{u_2}\right)
\]

---

#### 6. Derivation: Proof of Inverse Relation \(n \propto \frac{1}{u}\)

Let a physical quantity \(Q\) be represented by a standard length segment.

Suppose unit \(u_1 = 1\text{ m}\) and unit \(u_2 = 1\text{ cm}\). Since \(1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm}\):

\[
Q = 1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm}
\]

Here:

- \(n_1 = 1\), \(u_1 = \text{m}\)
- \(n_2 = 100\), \(u_2 = \text{cm}\)

Since \(u_1 > u_2\) \((1\text{ m} > 1\text{ cm})\), we observe \(n_1 < n_2\) \((1 < 100)\). Thus:

\[
n_1u_1 = n_2u_2 = \text{constant} \quad \Rightarrow \quad n \propto \frac{1}{u}
\]

---

#### 7. Meaning of Variables

- \(Q\): Measured physical quantity.
- \(n\): Pure number / numerical magnitude in a specific unit system.
- \(u\): Unit of measurement in that specific system.
- \(n_1, n_2\): Numerical values in systems 1 and 2 respectively.
- \(u_1, u_2\): Standard units in systems 1 and 2 respectively.

---

#### 8. SI Unit & Dimensions

- **Unit of a Physical Quantity:** Depends on the specific quantity (for example, metre for length, kilogram for mass, second for time).
- **Dimensions:** Physical quantities can be dimensional (for example, force \([MLT^{-2}]\)) or dimensionless ratios (for example, relative density, refractive index, strain).

---

#### 9. Physical Constants & Types of Physical Quantities

Physical quantities are classified into four structural categories:

1. **Dimensional Variables:** Quantities having dimensions and variable values (for example, velocity, acceleration, force, work).
2. **Dimensionless Variables:** Quantities having no dimensions but variable values (for example, strain, specific gravity, refractive index, plane angle).
3. **Dimensional Constants:** Quantities possessing dimensions with fixed universal values (for example, the universal gravitational constant \(G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{N m}^2\text{/kg}^2\), speed of light \(c\), and Planck's constant \(h\)).
4. **Dimensionless Constants:** Pure numbers or mathematical constants having neither dimensions nor units (for example, \(\pi\), \(e\), and numbers like 1, 2, and \(\frac{1}{2}\)).

---

#### 10. Graph / Diagram: Numerical Value \(n\) vs. Unit Size \(u\)

```text
  n (Numerical Value)
  ^
  |  *
  |   *
  |    *  Rectangular Hyperbola: n * u = Constant
  |      *
  |        *
  |           *
  |               *
  +-------------------------------------> u (Size of Unit)
```

- **Axes:** X-axis = size of unit \(u\); Y-axis = numerical value \(n\).
- **Shape:** Rectangular hyperbola in the first quadrant \((n\cdot u = k)\).
- **Slope:** \(\frac{dn}{du} = -\frac{k}{u^2}\). The slope is negative, confirming that as unit size increases, numerical magnitude decreases at an accelerating rate.
- **APPSC Trap:** A plot of \(n\) vs. \(\frac{1}{u}\) is a straight line passing through the origin with a positive slope equal to \(Q\).

#### 13. Common Confusions

| Concept A             | Concept B                  | Key Difference / APPSC Trap                                                                                                                                         |
| :-------------------- | :------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Physical Quantity** | **Non-Physical Quantity**  | Physical quantities can be measured objectively with instruments; non-physical entities cannot.                                                                     |
| **Unitless Quantity** | **Dimensionless Quantity** | A unitless quantity is always dimensionless. However, a dimensionless quantity can have a unit (for example, angle has unit radian, and solid angle has steradian). |
| **Mass**              | **Weight**                 | Mass is an intrinsic fundamental scalar quantity \([M]\); weight is a derived gravitational force \([MLT^{-2}]\).                                                   |
| **Scalar Quantity**   | **Vector Quantity**        | Scalars require magnitude only; vectors require magnitude, direction, and obey laws of vector addition.                                                             |

#### 15. Worked Examples

##### Worked Example 1.1 (System Conversion Model - APPSC Standard)

**Problem:** The density of mercury is \(13.6\text{ g/cm}^3\) in the CGS system. Convert it into SI units \((\text{kg/m}^3)\) using the fundamental measurement relation \(n_1u_1 = n_2u_2\).

- **Given:**
  - \(n_1 = 13.6\)
  - \(u_1 = \text{g/cm}^3 = \text{g}\cdot\text{cm}^{-3}\)
  - \(u_2 = \text{kg/m}^3 = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}\)
- **Required:** Numerical value in SI units \((n_2)\).
- **Formula:**

\[
n_2 = n_1\left(\frac{\text{g}}{\text{kg}}\right)^1\left(\frac{\text{cm}}{\text{m}}\right)^{-3}
\]

- **Substitution & Calculation:**

\[
n_2 = 13.6\times\left(\frac{1\text{ g}}{1000\text{ g}}\right)^1\times\left(\frac{1\text{ cm}}{100\text{ cm}}\right)^{-3}
\]

\[
n_2 = 13.6\times 10^{-3}\times 10^6 = 13.6\times 10^3 = 13600
\]

- **Final Answer:** Density of mercury in SI units = \(13600\text{ kg/m}^3\).
- **Common Trap:** Inverting the exponent for density \((-3)\) leading to a factor of \(10^{-6}\) instead of \(10^6\).

#### 16. Practice Numericals

##### Numerical 1 (Easy)

Express a speed of \(72\text{ km/h}\) in SI units \((\text{m/s})\).

- **Solution:**
  - Given: \(n_1 = 72\), \(u_1 = \text{km/h}\)
  - Formula: \(1\text{ km/h} = \frac{1000\text{ m}}{3600\text{ s}} = \frac{5}{18}\text{ m/s}\)
  - Calculation: \(n_2 = 72\times \frac{5}{18} = 20\text{ m/s}\)
  - Final Answer: \(20\text{ m/s}\)

##### Numerical 2 (Moderate - H.C. Verma Model)

If the unit of force is doubled, the unit of length is halved, and the unit of time is quadrupled, by what factor does the numerical value of work done change?

- **Solution:**
  - Work has dimensional formula \([W] = [ML^2T^{-2}]\).
  - Expressing force as \(F = [MLT^{-2}]\), work can be written as \(W = F\cdot L\).
  - Given new units: \(F' = 2F\), \(L' = \frac{1}{2}L\).
  - New unit of work: \(u' = F'\cdot L' = (2F)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}L\right) = F\cdot L = u\).
  - Since \(u' = u\), the numerical value remains unchanged.
  - Final Answer: The numerical value remains unchanged (factor = 1).

##### Numerical 3 (APPSC Level)

In a new hypothetical system of units, the unit of mass is \(10\text{ kg}\), the unit of length is \(100\text{ m}\), and the unit of time is \(1\text{ minute}\) \((60\text{ s})\). Calculate the numerical value of energy in this new system if 1 J is the original unit of energy.

- **Solution:**
  - Dimensional formula of energy = \([M^1L^2T^{-2}]\).
  - \(n_2 = n_1\left(\frac{M_1}{M_2}\right)^1\left(\frac{L_1}{L_2}\right)^2\left(\frac{T_1}{T_2}\right)^{-2}\)
  - \(n_1 = 1\text{ Joule}\), with \(M_1=1\text{ kg}\), \(L_1=1\text{ m}\), \(T_1=1\text{ s}\)
  - \(M_2 = 10\text{ kg}\), \(L_2 = 100\text{ m}\), \(T_2 = 60\text{ s}\)

\[
n_2 = 1\times\left(\frac{1\text{ kg}}{10\text{ kg}}\right)^1\times\left(\frac{1\text{ m}}{100\text{ m}}\right)^2\times\left(\frac{1\text{ s}}{60\text{ s}}\right)^{-2}
\]

\[
n_2 = \left(\frac{1}{10}\right)\times\left(\frac{1}{10000}\right)\times 60^2 = \frac{3600}{100000} = 0.036
\]

- **Final Answer:** \(1\text{ Joule} = 0.036\text{ new units}\).

#### 17. APPSC Level MCQs

##### MCQ 1 (Conceptual)

Which of the following statements is **INCORRECT** regarding physical quantities and units?

A. The numerical value of a physical quantity is inversely proportional to the size of the unit chosen.
B. A unitless quantity can never have non-zero dimensions.
C. A dimensionless quantity can never have a unit.
D. The physical magnitude of a quantity is invariant under a change of unit system.

- **Answer:** **C**
- **Explanation:** Option C is incorrect because a dimensionless quantity can have a unit. For instance, plane angle (ratio of arc length to radius) is dimensionless \([M^0L^0T^0]\) but has the SI unit radian.

##### MCQ 2 (Assertion-Reasoning)

- **Assertion (A):** The numerical value of energy in the CGS system (erg) is larger than that in the SI system (joule).
- **Reason (R):** Erg is a smaller unit of energy than joule, and for any physical quantity \(n \propto \frac{1}{u}\).

A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
B. Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
C. A is true but R is false.
D. A is false but R is true.

- **Answer:** **A**
- **Explanation:** Since \(1\text{ J} = 10^7\text{ erg}\), erg is a smaller unit. By the fundamental relation \(n\cdot u = \text{constant}\), a smaller unit corresponds to a larger numerical value.

##### MCQ 3 (Classification)

Which of the following contains ONLY dimensional constants?

A. Refractive index, strain, relative density
B. Universal gravitational constant, speed of light in vacuum, Planck's constant
C. Velocity, acceleration, force
D. Plane angle, solid angle, dielectric constant

- **Answer:** **B**
- **Explanation:** Gravitational constant \(G\), speed of light \(c\), and Planck's constant \(h\) all possess fixed values and non-zero dimensions, making them dimensional constants.
