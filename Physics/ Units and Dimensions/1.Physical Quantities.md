### 1. Concept & Definition
A **Physical Quantity** is any property of a material, object, or physical system that can be quantified and measured using an instrument, and whose magnitude can be expressed as a number multiplied by a unit.

Mathematically, any physical quantity $Q$ is represented as:
$$Q = n \cdot u$$
Where:
* $Q$ = Physical quantity
* $n$ = Numerical value (magnitude in chosen unit)
* $u$ = Unit of measurement

---

### 2. Physical Meaning
Physics is a quantitative science based on observation and measurement. To express the laws of nature in precise mathematical form, we must measure physical properties such as length, mass, time, temperature, force and energy.

A physical quantity is meaningful only when both its magnitude and unit are specified.

---

### 3. Fundamental Principle / Law
#### The Invariance Principle of Physical Quantities:
The magnitude of a physical quantity remains constant regardless of the system of units chosen for measurement:
$$Q = n_1 u_1 = n_2 u_2 = \text{Constant}$$

From this, the numerical value $n$ is inversely proportional to the size of the unit $u$:
$$n \propto \frac{1}{u}$$

*Interpretation:* The larger the unit chosen, the smaller will be the numerical value representing the physical quantity.

---

### 4. Classification of Physical Quantities

Physical quantities are broadly classified into **two primary categories** based on their mutual independence:

```text
                     PHYSICAL QUANTITIES
                             │
       ┌─────────────────────┴─────────────────────┐
       ▼                                           ▼
1. FUNDAMENTAL (BASE)                       2. DERIVED QUANTITIES
   QUANTITIES (7 Base)                         (Infinite, e.g., Force, Velocity)
       │
       ├─► (Historically: Supplementary Quantities: Plane Angle, Solid Angle)
```

#### A. Fundamental (Base) Quantities
These are physical quantities that are mutually independent of each other and cannot be defined or expressed in terms of any other physical quantity.

The **International System of Units (SI)** defines exactly **7 Base Quantities**:
1. **Length** ($L$)
2. **Mass** ($M$)
3. **Time** ($T$)
4. **Electric Current** ($I$ or $A$)
5. **Thermodynamic Temperature** ($\Theta$ or $K$)
6. **Amount of Substance** ($N$ or $\text{mol}$)
7. **Luminous Intensity** ($J$ or $\text{cd}$)

#### B. Derived Quantities
Physical quantities whose definitions and units are derived from combinations of one or more fundamental quantities using physical mathematical relations.

*Examples:* Speed, Velocity, Acceleration, Force, Work, Energy, Pressure, Density, Electric Potential, Magnetic Induction.

#### C. Supplementary Quantities (Historical / Special Classification)
Until 1995, CGPM classified two quantities as "Supplementary Quantities":
1. **Plane Angle** ($\theta$): Measured in Radian ($\text{rad}$)
2. **Solid Angle** ($\Omega$): Measured in Steradian ($\text{sr}$)

*Note:* In 1995, the CIPM reclassified plane angle and solid angle as **dimensionless derived quantities**.

---

### 5. Equation & Mathematical Representation

1. **General Measure Equation:**
   $$Q = n_1 u_1 = n_2 u_2$$
2. **Derived Quantity Dependency Equation:**
   $$[\text{Derived Quantity}] = [L]^a [M]^b [T]^c [I]^d [\Theta]^e [N]^f [J]^g$$
3. **Plane Angle** ($\theta$):
   $$d\theta = \frac{ds}{r} \quad (\text{Dimensionless}, \text{SI Unit: Radian})$$
4. **Solid Angle** ($\Omega$):
   $$d\Omega = \frac{dA}{r^2} \quad (\text{Dimensionless}, \text{SI Unit: Steradian})$$

### 10. High-Yield NCERT Facts
1. **SI System History:** Established by the 14th General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) in 1971.
2. **Base Unit Definitions:**
    * *Metre:* Defined in terms of the speed of light in vacuum ($c$) as the path length travelled by light in $1/299,792,458$ of a second.
    * *Second:* Defined via the caesium-133 atom's atomic clock transition frequency ($\Delta \nu_{Cs} = 9,192,631,770 \text{ Hz}$).
    * *Ampere:* Defined by fixing the elementary charge $e = 1.60217663 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$.
3. **Dimensionless Quantities with Units:** Plane Angle ($\text{rad}$) and Solid Angle ($\text{sr}$) have units but are dimensionless ($[M^0 L^0 T^0]$).
4. **Dimensionless Quantities WITHOUT Units:** Refractive index, Relative density, Specific gravity, Strain, Poisson's ratio ($\sigma$).

### 11. Common Confusions (DO NOT CONFUSE LIST)

#### A. Mass vs Weight
* **Mass** ($m$): Fundamental scalar quantity. Amount of matter in a body. SI Unit: $\text{kg}$. Remains constant everywhere in the universe.
* **Weight** ($W = mg$): Derived vector quantity. Gravitational pull exerted on a body by Earth or a celestial body. SI Unit: Newton ($\text{N}$). Changes with location ($g$).

#### B. Scalar Quantities vs Vector Quantities
* **Scalar Physical Quantities:** Possess magnitude only (e.g., Mass, Distance, Speed, Energy, Temperature, Pressure, Work, Charge).
* **Vector Physical Quantities:** Possess both magnitude and direction, and obey vector addition laws (e.g., Displacement, Velocity, Acceleration, Force, Momentum, Torque, Electric Field).

#### C. Supplementary Units vs Base Units
* Supplementary units ($\text{rad}, \text{sr}$) are dimensionless derived units, whereas Base units ($\text{m}, \text{kg}, \text{s}$, etc.) possess explicit fundamental dimensions.

### 12. APPSC Angle (Exam Patterns & Traps)
* **Trap 1:** Asking whether Electric Current is a scalar or vector. *Answer:* Electric Current has magnitude and direction, but it is a **scalar quantity** because it obeys scalar addition (algebraic addition), not vector addition.
* **Trap 2:** Confusing supplementary units with dimensionless quantities having no unit. Angle has no dimensions ($[M^0 L^0 T^0]$) but has a unit ($\text{radian}$).
* **Trap 3:** Asking for the SI unit of "Amount of Substance". *Trap Option:* Gram / Kilogram. *Correct Option:* Mole ($\text{mol}$).

### 13. Worked Examples & Numericals

#### Example 1 (Easy — Unit Conversion $n_1 u_1 = n_2 u_2$)
**Problem:** The density of iron is $7.8\,\text{g cm}^{-3}$ in CGS units. Find its value in SI units ($\text{kg m}^{-3}$).
* **Given:** $n_1 = 7.8$, $u_1 = \text{g cm}^{-3}$
* **Required:** $n_2$ in $u_2 = \text{kg m}^{-3}$
* **Formula:** $n_1 u_1 = n_2 u_2 \implies n_2 = n_1 \left(\frac{u_1}{u_2}\right)$
* **Substitution:**
  $$n_2 = 7.8 \times \left(\frac{1\,\text{g}}{1\,\text{kg}}\right) \times \left(\frac{1\,\text{cm}}{1\,\text{m}}\right)^{-3}$$
  $$n_2 = 7.8 \times (10^{-3}) \times (10^{-2})^{-3} = 7.8 \times 10^{-3} \times 10^6 = 7.8 \times 10^3$$
* **Final Answer:** $7800\,\text{kg m}^{-3}$.
* **Common Trap:** Forgetting that power $-3$ flips $10^{-2}$ to $10^{+6}$.

---

#### Example 2 (Moderate — Derived Quantity Formulation)
**Problem:** A physical quantity $P$ is given by $P = \frac{A^2 B}{C \sqrt{D}}$. If fundamental dimensions of $A, B, C, D$ are known, state the dimensional formula of $P$.
* **Given:** $A = [L]$, $B = [M]$, $C = [T]$, $D = [L^2]$
* **Required:** Dimensional formula of $P$
* **Formula:** $$[P] = \frac{[A]^2 [B]}{[C] [D]^{1/2}}$$
* **Substitution:**
  $$[P] = \frac{[L]^2 [M]}{[T] [L^2]^{1/2}} = \frac{[L^2] [M]}{[T] [L]} = [M^1 L^{2-1} T^{-1}] = [M^1 L^1 T^{-1}]$$
* **Final Answer:** $[M^1 L^1 T^{-1}]$ (which represents Linear Momentum or Impulse).

---

#### Example 3 (APPSC Level — Tricky Ratio)
**Problem:** If the unit of force is doubled, the unit of length is halved, and the unit of time is quadrupled, by what factor does the unit of energy change?
* **Given:** $F' = 2F$, $L' = \frac{1}{2}L$, $T' = 4T$
* **Required:** New unit of Energy $E'$ relative to $E$
* **Formula:** Energy $E = \text{Force} \times \text{Length} = F \cdot L$
* **Substitution:**
  $$E' = F' \cdot L' = (2F) \times \left(\frac{1}{2} L\right) = 1 \cdot (F \cdot L) = E$$
* **Final Answer:** The unit of energy remains **unchanged (Factor = 1)**.
* **Common Trap:** Students often blindly substitute into $[M L^2 T^{-2}]$ and get confused with mass changes instead of using direct force-length equivalence $E = F \cdot L$.

### 14. Practice Multiple Choice Questions (MCQs)

#### MCQ 1 (Conceptual)
Which of the following sets contains **ONLY** fundamental physical quantities in the SI system?
(A) Mass, Velocity, Time  
(B) Length, Electric Current, Amount of Substance  
(C) Force, Length, Temperature  
(D) Work, Luminous Intensity, Time  

* **Answer:** **(B)**
* **Explanation:** Length, Electric Current, and Amount of Substance are all among the 7 SI base quantities. Velocity, Force, and Work are derived quantities.
* **Difficulty:** Easy
* **Subtopic:** Physical Quantities (Base vs Derived)
* **NCERT Source:** Class 11 Physics Ch 2, Section 2.2

#### MCQ 2 (Tricky / Statement-Based)
Consider the following statements regarding Physical Quantities:
1. A physical quantity that is dimensionless can still possess a physical unit.
2. A physical quantity that has no units can still possess dimensions.
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
(A) 1 only  
(B) 2 only  
(C) Both 1 and 2  
(D) Neither 1 nor 2  

* **Answer:** **(A)**
* **Explanation:** Statement 1 is CORRECT: Plane angle has no dimensions ($[M^0 L^0 T^0]$) but has the unit radian. Statement 2 is INCORRECT: Any quantity that has no units is strictly dimensionless.
* **Difficulty:** Moderate / APPSC Level
* **Subtopic:** Physical Quantities & Dimensions
* **NCERT Source:** Class 11 Physics Ch 2, Section 2.2 & 2.8

---

#### MCQ 3 (Assertion & Reasoning)
* **Assertion (A):** Electric current is classified as a fundamental physical quantity in the SI system.
* **Reason (R):** Electric current possesses both magnitude and direction, obeying the parallelogram law of vector addition.

(A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
(B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
(C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
(D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.  

* **Answer:** **(C)**
* **Explanation:** Assertion (A) is TRUE: Electric current is one of the 7 base SI quantities. Reason (R) is FALSE: Electric current does NOT obey vector addition laws; it adds algebraically (scalar addition).
* **Difficulty:** APPSC Standard
* **Subtopic:** Physical Quantities & Scalar/Vector Classification
* **NCERT Source:** Class 11 Physics Ch 2 & Class 12 Physics Ch 3

---

#### MCQ 4 (Numerical / Unit Conversion)
If the numerical value of a physical quantity is $n_1$ in a unit system $u_1$ and $n_2$ in a unit system $u_2$, then which relation is always true?
(A) $n_1 u_1 + n_2 u_2 = 0$  
(B) $n_1 n_2 = u_1 u_2$  
(C) $n_1 u_1 = n_2 u_2$  
(D) $\frac{n_1}{u_1} = \frac{n_2}{u_2}$  

* **Answer:** **(C)**
* **Explanation:** The absolute magnitude of a physical quantity is invariant of the unit system chosen, so $Q = n_1 u_1 = n_2 u_2 = \text{Constant}$.
* **Difficulty:** Easy
* **Subtopic:** Physical Quantities Principles
* **NCERT Source:** Class 11 Physics Ch 2, Section 2.1
